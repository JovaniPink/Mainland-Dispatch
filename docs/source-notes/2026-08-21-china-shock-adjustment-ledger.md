## Editorial question and working answer

How does China's renewed manufacturing and export expansion travel through the
world economy, who receives its benefits, who bears its costs, and which policy
tools address which part of the adjustment?

The reviewed record supports a distributional answer. “China Shock 2.0” is a
fair name for a large, observable adjustment in production and trade, especially
where import exposure is rapid and locally concentrated. It becomes misleading
when used as a single-cause diagnosis, an aggregate verdict on trade, or a
ready-made case for one policy. China's weak household demand, property
adjustment, saving, industrial policy, productivity, import substitution,
export prices, and exchange rate do not have equal evidence or identical
effects. Foreign consumers can gain purchasing power; firms can gain cheaper
inputs; exposed workers and regions can face durable losses; and governments
can shift costs again through tariffs, subsidies, coordination, localization,
or retaliation.

## Evidence and decision labels

- **Measured:** a named statistical, regulatory, or research source publishes
  the quantity and method.
- **Modelled:** the result comes from an identified model or counterfactual,
  not a directly observed outcome.
- **Institutional assessment:** an institution applies judgment to measured or
  reported evidence; authorship and disagreement remain visible.
- **Official position:** a government states its diagnosis or defense; this is
  authoritative for that government's position, not independent proof.
- **Interpretation:** Mainland Dispatch synthesizes multiple records.
- **Scenario:** a conditional future path, never an observed result.
- **Retain:** the reviewed record supports the claim with its scope visible.
- **Qualify:** a narrower claim is supported after preserving source, method,
  denominator, period, or disagreement.
- **Exclude:** the record does not support publication as fact or the wording
  collapses distinct mechanisms.

### Product ideas retained from the memo

Four ideas fit Notebook Five's evidence model without changing its thesis:

1. **Show the denominator:** make the first/second-shock comparison expose the
   current unit, period, geography, and denominator instead of offering
   apparently interchangeable headline numbers.
2. **Follow the number:** let a chart datum reveal its source, dataset, metric,
   calculation, and caveat. This is a compact form of source lineage, not a
   claim that the site hosts a live database.
3. **Compare explanations:** map weak demand, productivity, industrial policy,
   credit, import substitution, prices, and currency to the observations each
   can and cannot explain.
4. **What would change the thesis:** name observable disconfirming conditions,
   including sustained consumption rebalancing, a durable surplus contraction,
   normalized utilization, and weaker export displacement after controlling for
   destination demand.

Living charts, automated updates, reader-built comparisons, time-travel maps,
prediction tracking, and a full source-lineage database remain deferred. They

## Audit one: how the adjustment travels

### 1. Domestic demand

The strongest macroeconomic record begins inside China. The IMF links the
property adjustment and local-government debt to weak private demand and
deflationary pressure. Its rebalancing analysis adds high saving and a weaker
safety net to the mechanism. The claim is not that Chinese households freely
chose a globally disruptive strategy. The distributional point is that
households can bear lower consumption and greater precautionary saving while
industrial production remains protected or expanded.

The social-protection claim needs a defined unit before publication. Health,
pensions, unemployment support, household registration, household wealth, and
out-of-pocket risk are not one measure. The episode can motivate the question;
the public answer must come from reviewed, component-specific evidence.

### 2. Industrial capacity

The evidence supports both capability and distortion channels. IEA data show
enormous production scale, lower costs, expanding trade, and uptake in emerging
markets. The IMF identifies state-led, debt-financed investment and
unwarranted support that can create misallocation and excess supply in some
tradable sectors. MOFCOM argues that innovation, productivity, global demand,
and comparative advantage better explain the same competitiveness.

The Notebook must not make “subsidized” the opposite of “innovative.” A firm or
sector can learn, innovate, scale, receive state support, face weak profits, and
still sell a useful low-cost product. The causal mix is the disputed object.

### 3. Trade balance

The Fed documents the asymmetry: export share rose from an already large base
while import share rose only modestly, and the surplus exceeded 1% of
rest-of-world GDP. The IMF frames the current account as saving minus
investment and attributes both Chinese surpluses and US deficits to domestic
policies. Setser's customs-data work argues that unusual gold imports and chip
prices obscure a still-rising manufacturing surplus.

Currency claims require qualification. The IMF supports a real exchange-rate
depreciation channel. Setser and Tordoir make a stronger intervention and
undervaluation argument. The Notebook may present those as related assessments,
but not as one uncontested measured fact.

### 4. External absorption

“Absorption” is not passive receipt of a single quantity. A destination can
import final goods, intermediate inputs, capital equipment, components for
local assembly, or products sold by a foreign-headquartered company operating
in China. The IEA shows Chinese EV exports shifting toward emerging markets and
localization expanding. The ECB shows why composition matters: final-goods
competition and intermediate-input cost relief can move production in opposite
directions.

Tariffs can redirect the route without changing the global balance. The Fed
finds the United States is the main regional exception to rising direct China
import penetration, while China's presence expanded elsewhere. That makes
destination exposure, transshipment, ownership, and local production separate
questions.

### 5. Local outcome

The first-shock literature justifies taking concentrated local losses
seriously even when aggregate trade gains exist. It does not justify assuming
that every exposed sector or place will repeat the US 2000s result. Current
outcomes must preserve the metric: employment, hours, wages, output, value
added, prices, firm entry and exit, investment, and fiscal transfers are not
substitutes.

The BLS record directly answers the reader question about output and jobs:
manufacturing output and labor input can diverge, but the broad assertion that
the United States makes “far more things than ever” is not supported by the
reviewed 2007–2026 aggregate index. Product mix, price adjustment, computers,
and physical volume require narrower series.

## Final thesis proposed for approval

“China Shock 2.0” describes an observable manufacturing and trade adjustment,
but not a single cause, outcome, or policy prescription. China's households
absorb part of it through weak consumption, precautionary saving, and property
risk. Chinese firms and workers absorb price competition, debt, consolidation,
and foreign barriers while gaining scale and export markets. Foreign consumers
and input users can receive cheaper goods and lower costs. Import-competing
workers, firms, and places can bear concentrated, persistent losses.
Governments then redistribute the adjustment again through tariffs, subsidies,
coordination, localization, diversification, and retaliation.

The useful question is therefore not whether the shock is simply “real” or
“fake.” It is which mechanism is operating, in which sector and period, who
absorbs the benefit or cost, and whether a proposed policy reaches that problem
without concealing the next transfer.
