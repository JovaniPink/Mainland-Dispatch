## Editorial question

Is China replacing exposure to the Strait of Hormuz with a new Arctic trade
route, or is Beijing assembling several narrower forms of resilience that move,
buffer, and redistribute different risks?

The reviewed record supports the second frame. China-linked state tanker fleets
are moving some loading activity outside the Gulf; estimated inventories and
lower import demand have created a substantial domestic buffer; and a Chinese
carrier has announced a small seasonal China–Europe container program through
the Northern Sea Route (NSR). These are not interchangeable interventions. The
tanker and inventory measures address crude supply. The NSR program addresses a
limited class of containerized exports. It cannot replace Hormuz oil or LNG
flows.

The submitted draft supplies a useful portfolio-strategy thesis, but it also
combines measured flows, unnamed-source reporting, operator promotion,
sanctions designations, intelligence allegations, and editorial inference as
if they had the same evidentiary status. This ledger separates those layers.

## Evidence labels

- **Measured:** a named statistical, regulatory, or tracking source publishes
  the quantity and method.
- **Reported:** a news organization attributes the claim to named data or
  unnamed sources; the underlying record is not fully public.
- **Announced:** a government, company, or operator states a plan or result
  about itself.
- **Assessment:** an institution or intelligence service supplies an analytical
  judgment that is not independently reproducible from the public record.
- **Interpretation:** the conclusion is an editorial synthesis, not a directly
  observed fact.
- **Not established:** the reviewed record does not support publication as a
  fact.

## Anchor evidence record

### Chokepoint scale and bypass capacity

1. [US EIA, _World Oil Transit Chokepoints_](https://www.eia.gov/international/content/analysis/special_topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints/)
   reports a first-half 2025 baseline of 20.9 million barrels per day (b/d) of
   oil through Hormuz, about 20% of global petroleum-liquids consumption and
   one-quarter of seaborne oil trade. It reports 11.4 billion cubic feet per day
   of LNG, more than 20% of global LNG trade. These are baseline flows, not a
   measured 2026 closure volume.
2. The same EIA record reports 4.2 million b/d through Bab el-Mandeb in the
   first half of 2025. Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb figures cannot simply be added:
   some Gulf cargo historically continued through Bab el-Mandeb and Suez, so
   the corridors contain overlapping routes.
3. [EIA's July 2026 bypass update](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67804)
   says Saudi Arabia's East–West Pipeline has 7 million b/d of capacity, with
   about 5 million b/d available for export after domestic-refinery deliveries,
   and the UAE's Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline has 1.8 million b/d. The
   approximately 6.8 million b/d combined export-capacity ceiling is not the
   same as realized throughput and is not a substitute for LNG.
4. [Saudi Aramco's first-quarter 2026 report](https://www.aramco.com/-/media/publications/corporate-reports/reports-and-presentations/2026/q1/saudi-aramco-q1-2026-interim-report-english.pdf)
   is the company source for the expanded 7 million b/d East–West maximum.
   The submitted 3.5–5.5 million b/d range is therefore outdated for this
   review date.
5. Current reporting describes Hormuz as effectively or functionally closed to
   normal commercial traffic, not physically sealed to every vessel. Some
   controlled, approved, or high-risk passages have occurred. “Shuttered” must
   therefore be qualified by traffic type, date, and source.

### Tanker risk relocation

6. Reuters reports that COSCO Shipping Energy Transportation and China
   Merchants Energy Shipping (CMES) stopped sending their tankers through
   Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb in late July. The two fleets together operate more
   than 100 very large crude carriers and carried about half of China's
   pre-conflict Middle Eastern crude imports, according to Reuters' sources.
7. Vortexa data cited by Reuters put ship-to-ship (STS) transfers involving
   China- or Hong Kong-owned vessels outside the Gulf above 600,000 b/d in June
   and July, compared with no recorded activity in April or May and less than
   30,000 b/d in January and February.
8. Reuters identified four COSCO supertankers and one CMES vessel loading via
   outside-Gulf STS transfers in July. Roughly two dozen additional vessels were
   scheduled for such loadings from August through mid-September. Scheduled is
   not completed.
9. Reuters assessed the Oman-to-China daily freight rate at about $140,000 and
   the margin at about $110,000 per day, compared with a pre-conflict daily
   profit of $30,000–$40,000 on a similar route. The draft should not label the
   $110,000 figure as both gross freight and profit.
10. [Reuters' related Saudi-loading report](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/saudi-arabia-resumes-oil-loadings-sales-inside-strait-hormuz-2026-08-18/)
    says Saudi Aramco offered Arab Medium and Arab Heavy cargoes to Asian
    refiners for STS loading off Fujairah. It also reports renewed inside-Gulf
    loadings during August 12–16. This shows that the adaptation moves a tanker
    handoff and some asset exposure; it does not remove the upstream Gulf leg.
11. The inference that Beijing has externalized some acute vessel risk is
    reasonable. The more specific submitted claim that smaller, non-Chinese
    regional shuttles necessarily perform every feeder leg is not established
    by the reviewed sources.

### Inventories, import demand, and the “swing consumer” thesis

12. [EIA's April 2026 China inventory estimate](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67504)
    puts combined government and commercial crude storage near 1.4 billion
    barrels in December 2025, after estimated additions averaging 1.1 million
    b/d during 2025. EIA estimates about 360 million barrels in government-held
    reserves and about 1 billion barrels in commercial storage.
13. EIA explicitly says China does not regularly publish comprehensive stock
    data. Its estimate combines official and third-party information and counts
    commercial holdings as potentially strategic in a way most cross-country
    comparisons do not. A table declaring Chinese “stockpiling dominance” over
    national strategic reserves would conceal incompatible definitions.
14. [EIA's July 31 review](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67905)
    says China imported 8.1 million b/d in the second quarter of 2026, 32% less
    than in the first quarter; May and June each fell below 8 million b/d for
    the first time since 2016.
15. [IEA's supply-readjustment analysis](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/how-global-oil-supplies-have-readjusted-to-help-fill-the-huge-gap-left-by-the-strait-of-hormuz-shock)
    calculates that China's crude imports fell about 4.6 million b/d between
    February and May. [IEA's July Oil Market Report](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-July-2026?mode=overview)
    estimates a 41-million-barrel Chinese stock draw in June. These are more
    defensible public anchors than the submitted 5.5 million b/d February–June
    claim.
16. The Council on Foreign Relations podcast describes China as a “swing
    consumer” and advances the estimate that its demand response took roughly
    $30 per barrel off Brent. Those are analytical judgments from the episode,
    not EIA or IEA measured series.
17. The words “weaponized,” “manipulate,” and “quietly winning” impute intent or
    declare an outcome. The public record establishes a large buffer, a sharp
    import reduction, and inventory drawdowns; it does not by itself establish
    a central order to manipulate world prices.
18. The submitted statement that Beijing halted all refined-product exports
    was not independently established during this review and is excluded from
    the working synthesis.

### Northern Sea Route: what is operating, announced, and limited

19. Sea Legend announced eight weekly China–Europe departures between August
    15 and October 3, 2026. [Xinde Marine's industry report](https://www.xindemarinenews.com/news/2077650383913914370),
    based on operator material, names the planned vessels and ports. This is a
    useful schedule record, not independent performance evidence.
20. The planned fleet ranges from 962 to 4,890 twenty-foot-equivalent units and
    carries ICE 1A, 1C, or 1D notation, according to a
    [second Xinde Marine fleet summary](https://www.xindemarinenews.com/news/2080682043257425922).
    The submitted names Dubai Tower, Riyadh Mukaab, Athens Odeon, Istanbul
    Bridge, Tiger Maanshan, Tiger Bintulu, and Tiger Lianyungang appear in that
    program.
21. Istanbul Bridge completed a China–UK Arctic trial in about 20 days in 2025.
    A completed trial is not proof that a 2026 weekly service has achieved
    schedule reliability. On this review date, the 2026 program should be
    called announced or scheduled unless vessel-specific departure and arrival
    evidence is added.
22. The reported 20-day Arctic transit, roughly 40-day Suez transit, and
    40–50-day Cape transit are indicative comparisons. Actual time depends on
    origin, destination, port calls, weather, ice, escort, and vessel class.
23. [The National Snow and Ice Data Center](https://nsidc.org/learn/ask-scientist/when-northeast-passage-open-ship-traffic)
    describes July through October as the broad navigation window and
    mid-September as generally optimal. It cautions that no part of the passage
    is guaranteed to be ice-free.
24. The route is a container service for selected manufactured cargo, not a
    crude-oil or LNG bypass from the Persian Gulf. Its eight mid-sized planned
    sailings have no valid unit conversion to Hormuz's daily liquid-energy
    throughput. The NSR is best described as a seasonal export-corridor hedge,
    not an energy panacea.
25. The Guardian reports operator interest in time-sensitive and
    temperature-sensitive cargo, including electric vehicles, batteries, and
    solar products. This is a target-market claim, not a manifested-cargo audit.
26. A claimed emissions reduction of as much as 50% comes from route advocates
    and operators. Shorter distance can reduce fuel use, but vessel efficiency,
    ice resistance, speed, escort, fuel, port calls, and system boundary control
    the result. Do not publish a single percentage as a measured route fact.

### Arctic governance, environmental risk, and the shadow fleet

27. [Bellona's 2025 NSR review](https://etc.bellona.org/publication/vessels-on-nsr/)
    identifies 100 vessels it classifies as sanctioned or shadow-fleet traffic,
    up from 13 in 2024 and close to one-third of the cargo vessels it tracked.
    Bellona is an environmental organization and sanctions monitor; its
    definitions and vessel matching should remain visible with the number.
28. Bellona documents risks associated with aging ships, incomplete ice class,
    opaque ownership or insurance, flag changes, and AIS gaps. Those risk
    factors support concern, but they do not yield a public probability that a
    collision or spill will occur.
29. [The International Maritime Organization](https://www.imo.org/en/mediacentre/hottopics/pages/polar-default.aspx)
    says its Arctic heavy-fuel-oil prohibition took effect July 1, 2024.
    Exemptions for some double-hull construction and coastal-state waivers can
    continue until July 1, 2029. “Banned in the Arctic” without the transition
    provisions is incomplete.
30. Russian law and administration govern NSR permits, route services, and
    icebreaker arrangements. Dependence on Russian permission and support is a
    material geopolitical exposure. The submitted phrases “absolute
    compliance” and “total authority,” and the allegation that Rosatom stopped
    publishing all vessel and incident data, need a direct legal and archival
    review before publication.
31. An Arctic spill would face severe darkness, cold, ice, distance, and
    response-capacity constraints. The submitted statement that conventional
    remediation would be “functionally useless” in every case is too absolute
    without a spill type, season, location, and response study.

### PRC-linked procurement for Iranian missile programs

32. [US Treasury, April 29, 2025](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0116),
    designated six entities and six individuals in Iran and the PRC for a
    network it said procured sodium perchlorate and dioctyl sebacate for the
    Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. The release names Saman Tejarat Barman,
    Shenzhen Amor Logistics, Dongying Weiaien Chemical, and Yanling Chuanxing
    Chemical among the participants.
33. [US Treasury, June 6, 2023](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1524),
    described a PRC- and Hong Kong-based network procuring centrifuges,
    accelerometers, gyroscopes, and radar-related components for Iranian
    defense entities. It names Zhejiang Qingji, Beijing Shiny Nights Technology
    Development, and Iranian defense attaché Davoud Damghani.
34. [US Treasury, March 30, 2022](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy0689),
    said Mohammad Ali Hosseini procured nitrile-butadiene-rubber processing
    equipment and a fluid-energy jet mill from suppliers in China for the IRGC
    Research and Self Sufficiency Jihad Organization and Parchin Chemical
    Industries.
35. [US Treasury's November 2025 transnational-network action](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0313)
    said a network had coordinated hundreds of metric tons of sodium chlorate,
    sodium perchlorate, and sebacic acid from China for Parchin Chemical
    Industries since 2023.
36. These releases establish the United States government's sanctions record
    and the named conduct it alleges or finds for designation purposes. They
    are not court judgments, and they do not independently prove that China's
    senior political leadership directed the transactions.
37. Treasury's 2023 release also says a participant used deceptive practices
    after PRC government warnings against dealing with designated Iranian
    firms. That detail cuts against treating every transaction as affirmative
    proof of a unified PRC state policy. The defensible formulation is
    “PRC-linked commercial and logistics networks identified by OFAC,” followed
    by the exact designation and end user.
38. Host-country enforcement, willful tolerance, regulatory failure, commercial
    evasion, and direct state tasking are different hypotheses. The current
    record does not resolve among them at the level claimed in the submitted
    “state negligence” or “CCP permissiveness” paragraphs.

## Working synthesis

The defensible synthesis has four distinct layers:

1. **Tactical shipping:** Chinese state-controlled tanker operators are keeping
   valuable VLCCs outside the highest-risk chokepoints and using STS loadings.
   This transfers and reprices exposure; it does not eliminate the Gulf leg.
2. **Domestic buffering:** Large, imperfectly measured inventories and a sharp
   reduction in crude imports gave China more room to sustain consumption than
   countries with smaller or less flexible buffers. Whether this constitutes
   deliberate global price management is an analytical question, not a
   measured fact.
3. **Export-route experimentation:** The 2026 NSR program is a seasonal,
   small-scale container experiment with potentially large time savings for
   selected cargo. It creates new Russian-governance, ice, environmental,
   insurance, and schedule risks and has no direct energy-bypass function.
4. **Conflict enablement:** US sanctions records support a durable problem of
   PRC-linked procurement for Iranian missile inputs. US and Ukrainian
   intelligence reporting supports concern about Russian information sharing.
   Neither record justifies collapsing commercial networks, state policy,
   intelligence support, and direct operational command into one claim.

The draft's strongest conclusion survives with narrower language: China is not
using one silver bullet. It is distributing exposure across inventories,
suppliers, transport handoffs, and a limited alternative export corridor. The
result is mitigation, not independence from maritime chokepoints.
